Docxray
  https://docxray.benrichardson.dev
  See what is still hiding inside a Word file before you email it. Reveals every tracked
  change with its author, every comment including the ones marked resolved, hidden and
  unreadable text, the document-management properties that name your client and matter,
  and — the one almost nobody knows about — the whole of every photograph that was

  uploaded.

Chunkforge
  https://chunkforge.benrichardson.dev
  Your assistant answers from the wrong page, and you cannot tell whether that is the model, the prompt, or the fact that the answer got sliced in half at chunk 47. Every guide hands you a number — 512 with 50 overlap, 1000 with 200 — and not one of them has seen your documents. Chunkforge measures it instead. Drop your own PDFs, type the queries that are actually failing, click the passage each one should have returned, and drag the slider: every move re-chunks, re-embeds and re-ranks the whole corpus in front of you, in about 220 milliseconds for three thousand chunks, because the scoring model is a lookup table rather than a network and no inference runtime ships at all. Three things make the numbers mean something. The count is in YOUR model's tokens — the real tokenizer.json for bge, MiniLM, gte, e5, nomic or OpenAI's cl100k, implemented exactly and checked token for token against a reference across an eleven-thousand-token document; a tokenizer that cannot be reproduced exactly is refused by name rather than approximated, because the artefact is a number you paste into your pipeline. The number of results is derived from a context budget you set, not fixed, because at a fixed top-10 a bigger chunk retrieves more text for free and "recall rises with chunk size" becomes arithmetic rather than a finding. And beside every score sits the chance level: exactly what random selection would score at that setting, in closed form. On the shipped sample, 96-token chunks score a perfect three out of three at a 43% chance level, and without that column that row reads identically to the genuinely good one. The recursive splitter is not an approximation of LangChain's — it is verified chunk for chunk against langchain-text-splitters itself across seven settings, including the degenerate ones where the library emits chunks larger than you asked for. It declares no winner, prints no percentage without its denominator, and shows nothing that looks like a score until you have marked something real. You leave with chunks.jsonl, the splitter config with your numbers filled in, and the caveats that would otherwise make it wrong the moment you paste it. The documents worth tuning retrieval over are the ones you cannot upload, and the queries are more revealing than the documents, so none of it leaves the tab.
  Tags: data-explorers, developer-tools, rag, retrieval, chunking, langchain, embeddings
  Repo: https://github.com/ben-gy/chunkforge

Runledger
  https://runledger.benrichardson.dev
  You spent more on an agent yesterday than you meant to. Your terminal told you the total and nothing else, and the obvious mental model — each thing that happened cost me once — is wrong, which is why reading the log does not help. Every turn re-sends the entire conversation, so a big tool result pasted into turn 5 of a 200-turn run is not paid for once; it is paid for again on all 195 turns after it. Priced across 25 real sessions, cache reads are 73.2% of the bill against 15% for writes and 12% for everything the model actually said. Repetition is not an anomaly to hunt down, it is the bill. So runledger is a carry ledger: drop your session files and every row is one turn, showing what it added and how many later turns re-read it, ranked by what it is still costing you. The numbers are not estimates. A session file reports its own token counts, and each turn's cached prefix turns out to be exactly the previous turn's prompt — measured across 12,475 turn pairs, the discrepancy is a median of two tokens — so the whole ledger is arithmetic over figures the run itself recorded. No tokenizer ships, because there is no public local Anthropic one and the file already holds the true counts. The first row is usually the surprise: the median session is carrying about 61,000 tokens before you have typed a word, and runledger sizes that block exactly, prices it exactly, and refuses to tell you what is inside it, because the system prompt and the tool schemas are not written to the log and guessing would be the one unforgivable thing. It also finds where your cache expired and the entire context was bought again at write prices, which is frequently the most expensive single event in a run. It shows its working: the decomposition should add back up to the cache-read total your session reported, and the tool prints the percentage it actually accounts for rather than asking to be believed. Your log contains every file the agent read — effectively your whole codebase — which is exactly why this is a browser tab and not an upload. A full run makes no network request at all.
  Tags: data-explorers, developer-tools, tokens, cost, claude-code, agents
  Repo: https://github.com/ben-gy/runledger

leakmap
  https://leakmap.benrichardson.dev
  You shipped a side project last night, and this morning you read that VITE_ and NEXT_PUBLIC_ variables are compiled into the JavaScript your users download — by design, and with no warning louder than a callout you skimmed. Now you cannot tell which of the seventeen values in your .env are sitting in a file anyone can fetch. The obvious answer is to upload your .env and your dist/ to a scanner, which is absurd on its face: uploading a secrets file to find out whether the secrets leaked is the disclosure. leakmap searches for your own values instead of a list of known key formats, which is the whole difference — when it says a value is present, it is present, and the finding is worth acting on. Getting that right took more than a string search. Vite 8 no longer parses .env with dotenv but with node's parseEnv, and the two disagree on real input, so both run and the union becomes the needle set; a value containing ${OTHER} is not in your bundle as written, so all three expansion engines run too; and exact matching inherits grep's recall, which found 9 of 14 planted secrets until the escape forms each minifier actually emits were enrolled alongside the literal. It also inherits grep's noise: a realistic .env over 5 MB of real bundles produced 76,738 matches, because "1" appears 37,781 times in ordinary code — so eight rules decide what is worth searching for, and every variable they set aside is listed by name with its reason and a button to search for it anyway. Then the second half: if your build shipped sourcemaps, leakmap finds them three separate ways (a glob misses an inline map, a content scan misses a hidden one, and a map nothing references at all is the loudest finding of the three), decodes the mappings itself, and hands back your original source tree. It never contacts OpenAI, Stripe or anyone else to ask whether a key still works, because that would mean transmitting your secret to a third party — the exact thing it exists to detect. Every value in every artefact is masked and there is no reveal button. It will not tell you that you are safe, and a test in the build fails if any string ever tries to.
  Tags: privacy, security, developer-tools, api-keys, sourcemaps, vite, nextjs
  Repo: https://github.com/ben-gy/leakmap

Charsight
  https://charsight.benrichardson.dev
  You cloned a repository, downloaded a README or were handed a "skill" file, and you are about to paste it into something that will act on your behalf. It looks completely ordinary. The Plane-14 tag characters mirror printable ASCII one for one and draw as literally nothing in every editor, terminal, browser and diff — and a language model reads them as ordinary instructions. Charsight finds them and prints the sentence they spell. It also decodes the byte payloads that ride on chains of variation selectors, catches the bidirectional overrides that reorder what you read without changing what the compiler reads, and spots the lookalikes: аррӏе is five Cyrillic characters that read as apple and are a different string entirely. Then it hands the file back byte-identical except for the spans you approved, with a findings file and a self-contained report you can attach to a ticket. A dozen sites will strip invisible characters out of text you paste into a box; none take a file, none take a folder, almost none tell you what the characters actually say. Severity here is a property of the occurrence, not the character: the zero-width joiner inside an emoji and the one inside a Latin identifier get opposite answers, Persian keeps the joiner it needs to be spelled correctly, and anything charsight can positively identify as legitimate is never touched. It will not tell you who or what wrote something, and a test in the build fails if any string ever suggests it could. While it works it makes no network request at all — the Unicode tables are compiled into the page, so a scan cannot leak even the fact that you ran one.
  Tags: privacy, security, unicode, prompt-injection, trojan-source, homoglyph, pdf
  Repo: https://github.com/ben-gy/charsight

Toolwright
  https://toolwright.benrichardson.dev
  You wired fourteen tools into an agent and OpenAI answered "'additionalProperties' is required to be supplied and to be false". You added it, and now Gemini answers 'Unknown name "additionalProperties"'. That is not bad luck, it is the shape of the whole problem: the single most-Googled repair in this space is itself a Gemini-breaking change, because additionalProperties:false is required by OpenAI strict, required by Anthropic strict, and does not exist in Gemini's parameters schema at all. Paste an MCP tools/list result, an OpenAI tools array in either shape, an Anthropic array or a Gemini functionDeclarations block, and Toolwright walks every node against eleven real targets — not three, because the tool shape and the accepted keywords vary independently, so a belt can be legal on one API of a provider and rejected by the other. You leave with the repaired files for each target, a llama.cpp GBNF grammar built by a first-party port that matches llama.cpp's own test corpus byte for byte, and a post-parse-checks.ts asserting exactly the constraints constrained decoding cannot enforce. It counts no tokens and fetches nothing: two of the three providers have no client-side tokenizer, so two of three columns would have been fabrications — and cutting them is what lets the page promise that nothing you paste ever leaves the tab. 188 of its 689 rule cells read "unknown", because that is what the documentation actually supports.

Backslide
  https://backslide.benrichardson.dev
  You changed one line of a system prompt, re-ran your eval, and the pass rate went from 94% to 91%. Now you have two files of a few hundred rows each and no idea which three per cent moved or why. Drop both into Backslide and it lines the rows up, checks only the things that can honestly be called worse — did it stop parsing, did it stop matching your schema, did an assertion your own harness already recorded flip, did it error or get cut off at the token limit — and hands back just the broken subset, with the reason attached and a report you can paste into the pull request. Everything happens in the tab, which matters here more than usual: an eval output holds your prompts, your customers' inputs, your model's answers and often a reference set you paid someone to write. Two things separate it from a spreadsheet formula. It aligns agent tool-call sequences rather than comparing them position by position, so one extra step at index 2 costs one gap instead of marking the next thirteen steps as broken — and an extra step is reported as a divergence, never a regression, because a longer path is not a worse path. And it refuses to guess: when two rows cannot be told apart it says so, and when nothing in your files can be objectively checked it says that too, instead of printing a confident "nothing got worse".
  Tags: llm, evals, regression-testing, diff, developer-tools, privacy
  Repo: https://github.com/ben-gy/backslide

Loraprep
  https://loraprep.benrichardson.dev
  Half of a LoRA training set is usually the same photo four times — burst frames off a phone, a shot re-saved at a lower quality, a thumbnail that came back from someone's email, three screenshots that got mixed in. Cleaning that up is most of the work of preparing a dataset and none of the interesting part, and the alternative is renting a cloud GPU to run a Python script you did not write, on photographs you would rather not upload: faces, client photography, a product that has not shipped. Drop the folder into Loraprep instead and it groups the near-identical frames on your own machine, picks the least-compressed in-focus copy of each, and hands back a zip laid out for kohya_ss and Hugging Face — with a report naming every image it set aside and the file it was judged against. What it will not do is the thing every AI deduplicator does: cluster CLIP embeddings and delete the extras. Measured on the standard LoRA reference sets, two genuinely different photographs of one subject score 0.99 similarity while a real duplicate that has been mirrored scores 0.88, so no cutoff separates them — the model ranks pairs for you to look at, and never removes anything itself.
